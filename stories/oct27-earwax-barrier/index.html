Earwax Turns Out to Be a Tiny Ant Obstacle Course

What happened

In an October 27 first-person explainer for The Conversation, Georgia Tech researcher David Hu described experiments published in Soft Matter showing that earwax is both slick and adhesive. The team used microscopy, rheology, ant-climbing tests and artificial earwax to study its porous, water-absorbing and viscoelastic behavior. Ants struggled both to climb wax-coated surfaces and to clean wax from their bodies.

Why it matters

The combined properties offer a mechanical explanation for how mammalian earwax could impede small invaders and may inspire lower-toxicity insect barriers. But this is a materials-and-ant study, not proof that earwax stops every insect, a finished repellent product or permission to put experimental substances in an ear. The paper also found that wax became brittle over roughly ten weeks, a possible route to self-removal.
