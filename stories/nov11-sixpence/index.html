A Crushed 1834 Sixpence Forces ‘Mint Condition’ Into Therapy

What happened

A ScienceDaily report published November 11 highlighted a laboratory investigation of a severely deformed 1834 William IV silver sixpence found in an assorted coin album at a Greater Manchester car-boot sale. The open-access npj Heritage Science paper reports microscopy, surface-sensitive chemical analysis, optical profilometry and digital imaging. The combined evidence was most consistent with severe compressive deformation during minting rather than ordinary wear, corrosion or one unconstrained later impact.

Why it matters

The conclusion is evidence-weighted, not a time machine: the measurements constrain the most plausible manufacturing explanation but do not reconstruct an exact press sequence or make every damaged coin a mint error. The paper also warns that surface EDX readings are affected by contamination and corrosion and should not be treated as a precise bulk-silver assay. Its broader value is a reusable, non-destructive workflow for separating manufacture, use, corrosion and later damage in unusual heritage objects.

The Blappos angle

The November 11 report, 1834 date, Greater Manchester find and measurement-constrained minting explanation end above. The Blappos interpretation: a sixpence survived 192 years, one steam press and an evidence review only to learn that mint condition was always an aspirational lifestyle brand.
