Swift Clocks Out in a Blaze After Nearly 22 Years

What happened

NASA's November 6 Astronomy Picture of the Day reported that the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory re-entered Earth's atmosphere on November 4 at 7:39 a.m. EST over North Sumatra, Indonesia, after nearly 22 years in orbit. The roughly 3,200-pound telescope launched in 2004 for a two-year mission and became a rapid-response observer of gamma-ray bursts and other violent cosmic events. NASA says the spacecraft burned up during re-entry; the illustrated satellite and visible breakup are editorial reconstruction, not an observation of recovered debris.

Why it matters

Swift's long life turned a short planned mission into two decades of coordinated space astronomy. It could detect a burst, calculate its position and quickly point its X-ray and ultraviolet/optical instruments at the same event, helping other observatories follow up. Its final clock is also a reminder that low-Earth-orbit missions eventually meet atmospheric drag: a failed 2026 commercial boost attempt could not extend Swift's orbit, and mission operators ended science work before the uncontrolled return.
