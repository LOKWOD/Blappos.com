Roman Britain's Frog Graveyard Turns Out to Be an Owl Buffet

What happened

November 17 coverage revisited a peer-reviewed investigation of the dense amphibian-bone deposit discovered during the A14 Cambridge-to-Huntingdon road project. The find was originally described publicly as more than 8,000 frog and toad bones; the paper reports 9,135 bones in the main gully, representing 72% of the full assemblage and at least 469 animals. Breakage patterns most closely matched short-eared owl pellets, while other evidence leaves room for a predator community that may also have included hen harriers.

Why it matters

The answer is more useful than a tidy whodunit. Bone measurements indicate many frogs died during autumn or winter in their first year, radiocarbon work places the deposit about 1,700 years ago, and pollen, charcoal and landscape evidence point to increasingly open Roman-period grassland. That supports an ecological reconstruction in which abundant young frogs became prey for grassland-hunting birds. It does not prove one owl made the pile, that every bone came from a frog or that people deliberately created the deposit.
