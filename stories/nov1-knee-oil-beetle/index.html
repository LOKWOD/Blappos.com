Wales Finds the Beetle With Knee Oil and a Bee Taxi

What happened

The Guardian reported November 1 that breeding by the rare short-necked oil beetle has been confirmed at Castlemartin in Pembrokeshire, nearly 60 years after the species was last known to breed in Wales. Adults can release an oily yellow-orange defensive fluid from their knee joints. The larvae have hook-like feet and hitch rides on solitary bees to reach their nests, where they feed on pollen stores; conservationists say only about one larva in 10,000 reaches adulthood. The beetle was found at the site in 2020, but the 2026 distribution of larvae supplied the stronger evidence of a successful hatch and breeding population.

Why it matters

A rediscovered adult is encouraging; larvae spread across suitable habitat are stronger evidence that a population is reproducing. The species also depends on the right flowers, bare ground and solitary-bee hosts, so its recovery reflects a connected habitat rather than one charismatic insect. 'Nearly 60 years' refers to confirmed breeding in Wales, not an absence of every beetle sighting during that period.
