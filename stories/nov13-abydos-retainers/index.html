Ancient Egypt Reopens a 5,000-Year-Old Royal HR Case

What happened

A ScienceDaily digest published November 13 highlighted Roselyn Campbell's open-access Frontiers in Environmental Archaeology study of 46 preserved crania from subsidiary burials around First Dynasty funerary enclosures at Abydos. Modern bioarchaeological and forensic examination identified perimortem, potentially lethal cranial trauma in 18 individuals—39% of the sample. The injuries provide new physical support for the long-standing hypothesis that some royal retainers were killed when rulers died, possibly to serve them in the afterlife.

Why it matters

The study recovers evidence and individuality from a legacy collection that earlier archaeology often treated as a footnote to royal tombs. Its claim ceiling is crucial: the 46 crania represent only about 5% to 15% of the burial population at each enclosure, many remains were lost, damaged or coated in paraffin, and most postcranial remains are unavailable. The trauma supports sacrifice for many examined people; it does not prove that every subsidiary burial was a killing or reveal exactly how people without observable cranial trauma died.
