Prehistoric Britain Builds a Three-Kilogram Axe to Impress the Group

What happened

New Scientist reported on November 9 that unusually large British ficron handaxes may have served a social or display role more than 300,000 years ago. The peer-reviewed study compared 817 ficrons and other handaxes from seven southern British sites; seven giant examples were at least 22 centimeters long, some reached roughly 30 centimeters and three kilograms, and the giants were unusually symmetrical and straight-edged. In controlled rope-cutting trials, replica giants took about 22 percent longer than ordinary ficrons.

Why it matters

The work combines morphometric comparison with experimental archaeology: giant replicas were difficult to make, took roughly 45 minutes rather than 20 to 30 minutes for ordinary examples, and offered no clear cutting advantage in the tested task. That supports the authors' proposal that the objects helped display skill or structure social relationships, but it does not prove a single ceremonial use, identify an individual maker's motive or show that every giant handaxe was impractical in every possible task.
