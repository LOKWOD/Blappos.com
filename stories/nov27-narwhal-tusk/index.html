The Narwhal's Spiral Tusk Has a Second Spiral Going the Other Way

What happened

A November 27 ScienceDaily digest revisited an August 18 Paul Scherrer Institute study that used X-ray tensor tomography at three synchrotron facilities to map a narwhal tusk from the millimeter scale down to nanometers. The Nature Communications paper reports that the tusk's outer cementum and inner dentin contain microscopic fibers arranged in opposing helices. The full tusk can grow to roughly two meters while remaining a long, straight biological composite rather than twisting into a corkscrew.

Why it matters

The opposing architecture gives materials researchers a cross-scale example of how a natural composite can combine stiffness, flexibility and resistance to torsion. The scans establish structure; they do not by themselves prove every biological job of the tusk, settle whether signaling, sensation or competition matters most, or turn one tooth into a manufacturing recipe. The November 27 digest date and the August research-publication date are separate clocks.
