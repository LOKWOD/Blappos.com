Two solar eruptions merged on the way to Earth and helped paint auroras across Britain

What happened

The Scottish Sun reported on October 11 that auroras had been photographed across Britain overnight, as far south as Kent and Surrey. NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center had forecast a G2 geomagnetic storm for October 11 after a faster coronal mass ejection launched October 8 was expected to catch and merge with a slower one launched October 7; NOAA's live aurora dashboard later recorded a 24-hour peak of Kp 6, which corresponds to G2 conditions.

Why it matters

The spectacle is the visible end of a Sun-to-Earth chain with practical consequences. NOAA classifies G2 as moderate: the same disturbance that sends charged particles into the upper atmosphere can require voltage corrections, affect high-latitude radio propagation and alter spacecraft operations. A wide aurora is therefore both a sky show and evidence of monitored space weather.
