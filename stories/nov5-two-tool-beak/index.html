Hawaiʻi Gives One Bird Two Union Jobs and No Woodpeckers

What happened

The Field Museum reported on November 5 that a new Ornithology study gives the most detailed anatomical account yet of the critically endangered ‘akiapōlāʻau. Its short, stout lower bill gouges into wood while its long, slender, down-curved upper bill probes for insect prey. Comparisons of museum specimens also found hindlimb and femur features consistent with bracing the whole body during gouging, helping the honeycreeper fill a woodpecker-like niche on an island chain without native woodpeckers.

Why it matters

The work connects internal anatomy with behavior that researchers can still observe in a living species: a material-flexibility trade-off in the upper bill, stronger ankle dorsiflexion and a low center of mass all support specialized foraging. It also sharpens the conservation stakes. The Field Museum says fewer than 2,000 birds remain, all on the northeastern slope of one mountain on Hawaiʻi Island. The paper describes function from anatomy and observation; it does not claim the bird literally operates interchangeable tools.
