A Bronze Age Urinary Stone Survives 5,300 Years

What happened

Live Science reported on November 3 that researchers identified a 21-by-17-millimeter calcified stone among the hand bones of a man buried at the Ipatovo-5 cemetery in the North Caucasus about 5,300 years ago. The peer-reviewed study used micro-CT, spectroscopy, diffraction and chemical analysis; carbapatite and whitlockite supported the conclusion that the object was a urinary stone of infectious origin. The man was about 40–49 years old and also showed healed fractures and extensive joint degeneration.

Why it matters

The chemistry can identify a urinary stone and evidence of a long-running infection, but it cannot locate the stone with certainty in the kidney versus bladder, name the organism that caused the infection or prove that the condition caused the man's death. The paper was published September 30; November 3 is the public-news clock. Those limits matter because a vivid archaeological object can easily be made more medically precise than the surviving evidence allows.
